# Related methods and resources

Methods that fit the same job as the skill (see what a future would be like before building it), grouped by what they add. Citations are from memory; check editions and links before quoting them.

## The two methods the skill is built on

- **Working backwards / PR-FAQ.** Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, *Working Backwards* (2021), chapters on the PR/FAQ and the six-page narrative. The press release fixes the customer benefit; the internal FAQ forces cost, risk and "why now" answers.
- **Design fiction.** Bruce Sterling coined the term; Julian Bleecker, "Design Fiction: A Short Essay on Design, Science, Fact and Fiction" (2009). Near Future Laboratory, *The Manual of Design Fiction* (Bleecker, Foster, Girardin, Nova, 2022) and their *TBD Catalog* (a fake product catalog from a near future). Useful for the diegetic-artifact formats.
- **Diegetic prototypes.** David Kirby, "The Future Is Now: Diegetic Prototypes and the Role of Popular Films in Generating Real-World Technological Development" (*Social Studies of Science*, 2010). Why showing a thing working in a story persuades.

## Speculative and futures methods

- **Speculative design.** Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby, *Speculative Everything* (2013). The futures cone (possible, plausible, probable, preferable) is the source of the skill's "futures cone" lens. The cone itself traces to Hancock and Bezold (1994) and Joseph Voros (2003).
- **Scenario planning.** Peter Schwartz, *The Art of the Long View* (1991); Shell's scenario practice. Useful when the question is "which future should we build for", not "what does this product look like".
- **Backcasting.** John Robinson's work from the 1980s on energy futures. Start from the desired end state and work back to today's first step; close to how the skill's "order of work" is written.
- **Three Horizons.** Bill Sharpe, *Three Horizons: The Patterning of Hope* (2013). For placing a design between today's system and a different one.
- **Futures wheel.** Jerome Glenn (1971). Map second- and third-order effects of a change; a quick add-on to the FAQ's "what could go wrong".

## Product and engineering methods

- **Pre-mortem.** Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem" (*Harvard Business Review*, 2007). The skill's pre-mortem lens.
- **Shape Up.** Ryan Singer, *Shape Up* (Basecamp, 2019, free online). Pitches with appetite, rabbit holes and no-gos: a good graduation format when the next step is scoping a build.
- **Readme Driven Development.** Tom Preston-Werner (2010 blog post). Write the README first; the natural rendered end state for libraries and CLIs.
- **Design docs and RFCs.** Google-style design docs; the Rust RFC template (motivation, guide-level explanation, alternatives, unresolved questions). The guide-level explanation is a form of working backwards.
- **Architecture Decision Records.** Michael Nygard (2011). Good graduation output for decisions settled during iteration.
- **Jobs to be done.** Clayton Christensen; Bob Moesta, *Demand-Side Sales 101* (2020); job stories from Intercom. Checks the press release against a real job.
- **Opportunity solution trees.** Teresa Torres, *Continuous Discovery Habits* (2021). Links the outcome to candidate solutions and the experiments that test them.
- **Assumption mapping.** David Bland and Alex Osterwalder, *Testing Business Ideas* (2019). Sort assumptions by importance and evidence; test the important, unproven ones first.
- **Wardley mapping.** Simon Wardley's book and blog (free online). Places components on an evolution axis; maps well onto works today / planned / proposed.
- **Story mapping.** Jeff Patton, *User Story Mapping* (2014). For turning the fiction's scene into a release plan after graduation.
- **Testing with people.** Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* (2013): how to show a design fiction to users without leading them. Wizard of Oz prototyping: fake the system behind a real interface to test the experience before building it.

## Examples worth studying

- Apple's *Knowledge Navigator* video (1987): a design fiction that shaped expectations for decades.
- Bret Victor, "The Future of Programming" (2013): a talk performed as if in 1973, a design fiction about the present. Also "Inventing on Principle" (2012) and his explorable explanations.
- Ink & Switch essays (for example "Local-first software", 2019): research essays that pair a vision with prototypes and an honest list of what's unsolved, close to the skill's reality check.
- Superflux (design studio): speculative work for governments and companies, often built as physical artifacts from a future.
- Near Future Laboratory's *TBD Catalog*: the fake-catalog format at full length.

## Ideas for the skill itself

- **A gallery of past explorations.** Keep each exploration's artifact URL and status in one index page (or this research repo's README) so later explorations can reuse sections and styling.
- **A shared stylesheet.** Publish the page skeleton (status chips, margin notes, light and dark tokens) once and copy it into new explorations, so drafts start looking finished.
- **Reviewer pass.** Before each republish, re-read the page as a skeptic: every "works today" has a source, every illustrative number is labelled, every open question is still open.
- **Evals.** Run the skill on two or three old ideas with known outcomes and compare the reality check against what actually happened.
